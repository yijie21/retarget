Do As I Do — where is the actual novelty? “Do as I Do: Dexterous Manipulation Data from Everyday Human Videos” (Paliwal, Etukuru, Liang, Abbeel, Shafiullah, Malik). Your hunch was that reconstruction is just off-the-shelf parts glued together and retargeting is just SPIDER. Here's how that holds up against the paper and the code in /home/user2/code/do-as-i-do.

Short answer: your instinct is half right.

The building blocks really are off-the-shelf, and the retargeter really is SPIDER (the repo literally says "built on SPIDER"). But the novelty isn't supposed to be the blocks — it lives in the seams, and the seams are real, non-trivial, and ablation-validated:

  • Reconstruction: turning a single-frame 3D model (SAM-3D) into a temporally-coherent 6-DoF video tracker via guided diffusion, plus a hand-anchored metric-scale trick that resolves monocular scale ambiguity. That's a genuine algorithmic idea, not glue.
  • Retargeting: three robustification components bolted onto SPIDER (warmup, force perturbation, transition/contact reward) that make it survive noisy reconstructed references — SPIDER alone scores 25%, with these 71%.
  • The "playbook": the empirical finding that only ~4% of internet clips are usable — a contribution in its own right.

So it's best read as a systems paper with two algorithmic seams, claiming “the first pipeline from an internet video to real dexterous-hand rollouts.” Whether that's "innovative enough" is a taste call — but "just a combination" undersells where the work actually is.

Novel idea #1 — repurpose a single-frame 3D model into a video tracker

SAM-3D reconstructs an object from one image. Run it per-frame and you get a different, jittering shape every frame (temporal incoherence). The paper's move: fix the object's shape at an anchor frame, then optimize only its pose across frames by steering SAM-3D's diffusion sampling — blending the diffusion velocity toward (a) the canonical anchor shape and (b) the previous frame's pose. The guidance strength isn't a fixed hyperparameter; it's adapted from the object's 2D rotational velocity measured by TAPIR (so a fast spin loosens the prior, a still object tightens it — avoiding both over-rigidity and spurious flips).

📂 Code evidence: scripts/tapir_velocity_tracking.py tracks the object with BootsTAPIR and "estimates translation + rotation velocity via SVD rigid fitting" — that velocity is exactly the adaptive-guidance signal. The guided sampler itself is in the modules/Fast-SAM3D/track_object.py fork (the "guided pose prediction" stage). (I read the first-party velocity script directly; the diffusion sampler lives in the submodule and is described by the paper/README.)

Novel idea #2 — get metric placement from a monocular video

Monocular reconstruction has an unknown global scale — you can't tell a small mug up close from a big mug far away. The trick: the HaWoR hand is metric (real MANO size), so use it as a ruler. Compare the hand's depth in the MoGe pointmap vs. its true HaWoR depth to recover the pointmap-to-real scale, then place the object metrically relative to the hand.

📂 Code evidence (read directly): scripts/optimize_translation_scale.py — "The anchor hand mesh from HaWoR is treated as ground truth … compute the pointmap-to-real scale factor per frame … then use the scaled hand-to-object vector to place the object." Steps: k = h_real_z / h_pm_z, obj_pos = h_real + k·(o_pm − h_pm). This is a clean, real contribution — not stitching.

Verdict on reconstruction: ~6 of 8 components are off-the-shelf, so calling it "a combination" is fair at the parts level. But the two NEW pieces are what make it work as a tracker and what the paper uses to beat FoundationPose / Any6D. The contribution is real; it's just concentrated.

3. Retargeting — yes, it is SPIDER… plus three things that matter

⚙️ You're correct: retargeting/README.md states "This codebase is built on SPIDER." The core — sampling-based MPC physics optimization in MuJoCo Warp (stage 5) — is SPIDER's framework, unchanged in spirit. The novelty is a robustification layer for noisy, reconstructed input (SPIDER was built for clean MoCap).

SPIDER assumes a fairly clean reference trajectory. A reference reconstructed from RGB video is noisy: the initial grasp may interpenetrate, contacts drift, pick/place moments are imprecise. Three additions fix exactly those failure modes:

① Warmup init-failure fix

Before tracking starts, prepend a phase that holds the object fixed (weld constraints + zero gravity) and lets the robot hand reposition from an open pose into a valid grasp, interpolating the reference in over a few steps — instead of snapping to a possibly-penetrating first frame. No grasp-specific heuristics needed.

📂 optimize_physics.py: warmup_analytical_init (translate wrist along −palm_normal, open fingers), warmup_weld_eq_ids + "zero gravity for N steps", warmup_ref_interp for base & fingers. Config: warmup_duration, warmup_min_clearance, warmup_ramp_duration in override/do_as_i_do.yaml. Paper ablation: 25% → 66% success from warmup alone.

② Random force/torque perturbation grasp-robustness

During rollouts, apply stochastic disturbances to the object so the optimizer is pushed toward grasps that actually hold under perturbation (sim-to-real style). Crucially, perturbations are gated to fire only deep inside a stable hold, never during reach-in or set-down.

📂 Config: num_perturb_samples, perturb_force_scale, perturb_torque_scale, perturb_prob, perturb_continue_prob. Gating: utils/in_hand.py → erode_mask() — "disturbances fire only well inside a stable hold."

③ Transition / contact reward pick-place fix

Detect the manipulation phase per frame — object in-hand vs. resting on a pedestal/floor — and reward the correct contact state in each: hand-object contact during in-hand phases, object-floor/pedestal contact during rest. This stops the classic failure where the robot "almost" picks up or drops early.

📂 utils/in_hand.py (read in full): compute_in_hand_mask, compute_near_floor_mask, compute_near_pedestal_mask (finite-cylinder footprint test), consumed by optimize_physics.py → _gate_state_lane() which labels each frame 1=in-hand / 2=resting / 0=neither. This is ~300 lines of real phase logic, not a stub.

Verdict on retargeting: the engine is SPIDER — you're right. But these three components are the difference between SPIDER failing on video-derived references (25%) and the system working (71%). They target reconstruction noise specifically, which is the whole point of the paper.

4. The third contribution you may have skipped: the “playbook”

A genuinely useful empirical result, separate from any algorithm: of ~2,000 clips from the 100DOH internet-video dataset, only ~9% contain meaningful hand-object interaction and only ~4% survive quality checks for reconstruction (hand/object leaving frame, shot boundaries, excessive camera motion, SAM-3D failures). They frame this as a ~20× penalty for not preprocessing internet video properly. For anyone planning to "just use internet videos," this is a concrete, money-saving finding.

6. So — is it innovative? An honest take

If you measure novelty by "new model architectures": no — almost every model is borrowed, and the retargeting engine is SPIDER. Your coarse read is defensible at this level.

If you measure novelty by "new capability + the ideas that unlock it": yes — it's the first end-to-end internet-video → real dexterous-robot rollout pipeline, and it required two real algorithmic inventions (guided-diffusion 6-DoF tracking; hand-anchored metric scale) plus three reconstruction-noise robustifications to SPIDER. The ablations show those aren't cosmetic.

This is a classic, legitimate systems-paper shape: the value is in making the whole chain work on messy real data, and the "innovation" is distributed across the seams rather than sitting in one headline module. If you're judging it for a review or deciding whether to build on it, the fair critique isn't "it's just a combination" — it's "are the seam-level contributions (guided tracker, hand-anchored scale, the three SPIDER add-ons) strong enough on their own?" — and the ablation numbers are the evidence to argue about.
